About

T.R. Dailey

T.R. Dailey spent eleven years reading other people’s mail, forty years too late. The novels came out of the habit.

Dailey grew up on the wrong side of a coastal town that photographed beautifully and kept to itself. Her grandfather ran a hardware store; her mother kept the books for half the businesses on the same street. Dailey has said she learned more about her neighbours from those ledgers than from twenty years of living among them — that a column of figures will tell you who was struggling, who was proud, and who was quietly covering for someone else, long before anybody says a word out loud.

She studied marine biology, briefly and badly, then archival science, which stuck. For eleven years she catalogued donated papers for a regional historical society: shoeboxes of letters, deeds with the corners eaten away, diaries whose most interesting years had been razored out. It was steady work and it suited her.

Her debut, The Weight of Unspoken Tides, began as a single index card describing a box of journals found beneath a floor. The Guest List for the End of the World came from a real invitation to a real island, which she declined. A Short History of the Duplicate started as an argument with a friend about whether grief can be outsourced. Her fourth novel, The Unraveling of August Finch, returns her to archives, ledgers, and the particular cruelty of a well-kept record.

Reviewers reach for words like quiet and unsettling, which she takes as a compliment. Her books are not about violence so much as about maintenance — the ongoing daily effort a family or an institution puts into keeping a story upright. She is interested in the moment that effort stops, and in who happens to be standing nearest when it does.

She writes in the mornings, in longhand, in cheap notebooks she refuses to number. Afternoons are for typing up whatever survived. She still takes occasional appraisal work for private collections, mostly because it gets her into other people’s attics.

Dailey lives on the same stretch of coast she grew up on, in a house with a bad roof and a very good view. She shares it with a retired greyhound named Ledger, who is not named after anything in particular, whatever she may tell interviewers.

A note from the author

People ask what the books have in common, and for years I gave a different answer every time. Here is the honest one. I am not interested in the secret. I am interested in the arrangement everyone made around it — who agreed to stop asking, who benefited, and what it cost the one person who never got a vote.

That is the whole engine. Everything else is weather and furniture.

— T.R.D.
